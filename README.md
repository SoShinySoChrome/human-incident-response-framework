# The Human Incident Response Framework

This repository contains the **Human Incident Response Framework**, a practitioner wellbeing framework for cybersecurity operations. It adapts stress models from the US military, social work, emergency medicine, and child welfare into vocabulary that fits security work, and gives practitioners, peer responders, and team leads a shared severity language, a check-in protocol, and an escalation path.

## Why This Exists

Cybersecurity has one word for what this work does to people, and it's the wrong word for most of it.

Four distinct injuries get filed under "burnout." **Burnout** is workload injury, and rest fixes it. **Secondary traumatic stress** comes from exposure to what happened to other people, and rest does nothing for it. **Vicarious trauma** is a slow change in worldview accumulated across years. **Moral injury** comes from being party to something you believe was wrong, or being stopped from doing what you believed was right.

Each has a different cause, a different signal, and a different fix. Only one responds to time off. Calling all four burnout means three of them get the wrong treatment, and the people carrying them conclude that nothing works.

Adjacent professions solved this decades ago. This framework borrows their work.

## Which One Do I Want?

**Start with the Field Guide** if you're an individual practitioner, a peer, or a team lead who wants to be useful to someone next week. It works without a budget, a mandate, or anyone above you agreeing to anything.

**Read the full Framework** if you're assessing a team's exposure by role, building a program, or making the case to leadership. It contains the organizational layer, the validated assessment instruments, and the role-by-role map.

Both carry the complete sources section. Neither requires the other.

## Getting Started

### Before you use any of it

Read the document first, then get trained. Two bodies of work sit underneath everything in the framework, and they're structured differently from each other.

- **[Stress First Aid](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/type/stress_first_aid.asp)** — a published model rather than a certification. The manuals are free from the National Center for PTSD. Instruction is available from several institutions, including Duke University's School of Nursing and the Schwartz Center for Compassionate Healthcare.
- **[Mental Health First Aid](https://www.mentalhealthfirstaid.org)** — a formal certification course delivered by licensed providers, free in many jurisdictions. Outside the US, find your country's program at [mhfainternational.org](https://mhfainternational.org).

The framework gives you vocabulary and a protocol. It can't give you the judgment that comes from training.

### Recognize

Read Part 1. It defines four severity zones — Ready, Reacting, Injured, Crisis — and gives five worked examples of practitioners moving through them. This is the recognition half, and most readers get the most out of the vignettes.

### Understand the exposure

Read the exposure drivers. Six things determine why one seat costs more than another: content type, decision weight, institutional friction, cumulative dose, adversarial engagement, and secrecy. The full framework scores roles against them. The Field Guide gives you the drivers alone, which is enough to work out why a particular seat is hard.

### Build a baseline

Read The Cadence. Structured, recurring contact outperforms an open door in every field that has measured it. The baseline you're building is something you hold, not something you file — that distinction is covered in the section, and it matters both for trust and for data protection.

### Check in

Read How to Check In. It walks the seven C's — Check, Coordinate, Cover, Calm, Connect, Competence, Confidence — with what to say, what not to say, and a worked example of a peer responder running the whole protocol.

### Know when it's past you

Read Escalation. It covers the thresholds for handing off, what escalation is and isn't, and how to route someone without turning it into surveillance or a disciplinary record.

### Route

Part 7 in the framework, Part 6 in the Field Guide, lists crisis lines, practitioner-specific support, and the validated instruments, with links.

## Target Audience

- **Practitioners** in high-exposure roles: threat intelligence, incident response, digital forensics, security operations, vulnerability research, trust and safety.
- **Peer responders** — anyone who wants to be useful to a colleague and doesn't know how to start.
- **Team leads and managers** who can see something is wrong and have no vocabulary for it.
- **CISOs and security leaders** building a program, using the organizational layer in the full framework.

## What It Can't Do

This is a detection aid. It isn't a safety system and shouldn't be treated as one.

People can be in serious trouble with none of these indicators present. Someone can hold a normal conversation, hit every deadline, answer a check-in honestly by their own reckoning, and still be nowhere near all right. Someone can also decline to tell you anything, and that's their right.

Peer support sits alongside clinical care and crisis services. It replaces neither, and it doesn't turn a colleague into a safety net.

**If you are worried about someone right now, don't run a protocol.** Use the resources at the top of this page.

## Contributing

Corrections, disagreements, adaptations, and arguments about where this gets something wrong are all welcome. Open an issue.

Two things worth knowing first. Every empirical claim has been checked against its primary source, and several were cut or rewritten when they didn't survive that check — so if you think a citation is wrong, please say which one and what it should say. And the role-to-injury mapping is explicitly clinical inference rather than empirical finding; challenges to it are the most useful thing anyone can send.
